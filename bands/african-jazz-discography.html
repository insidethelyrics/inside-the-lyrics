THE AFRICAN JAZZ ARCHIVE

African Jazz Discography

A working catalogue of songs, recordings and musical milestones associated with Grand Kallé and African Jazz.

About This Catalogue

African Jazz, led by Joseph Athanase Tshamala Kabasele, popularly known as Grand Kallé, produced an important body of recordings that helped shape modern Congolese rumba.

This page organises selected known titles by historical period. It is a developing research catalogue rather than a complete original discography. Some recordings appeared on different labels, formats and later compilations. Spellings, dates and credits may therefore differ between surviving records.

A song listed here should not automatically be interpreted as a separate album release. Many titles were originally issued as individual recordings or on 78 rpm and 45 rpm discs.

1950–1956: Early Recordings

The earliest recording years were important in the development of Kabasele's musical identity. His early work included studio collaborations before African Jazz became firmly established.

  • Valerine Regina – an early recording associated with Kabasele.
  • Kale Kato – an early title later revisited in subsequent sessions.
  • Parafifi – an early recording title, also revisited later.
  • African Jazz – a title associated with the orchestra's early catalogue.

Editorial note: Early studio recordings should not all be treated as recordings by a fully established African Jazz lineup.

1957–1959: The Esengo Years

Following the closure of Opika, African Jazz recorded at Esengo. This period brought new collaborations and important changes in the orchestra's sound and personnel.

  • Baila – associated with Jean-Serge Essous and the Esengo period.
  • Tujala Tshibemba
  • Mokonzi Ya Mboka
  • Tembe Nye

The Esengo recordings reflect the creative exchanges between African Jazz and musicians from other Congolese and Brazzaville musical groups.

1960: The Brussels Recordings

African Jazz travelled to Belgium during the Congolese Round Table Conference. The orchestra's performances and recordings in Europe became a defining moment in its history.

  • Indépendance Cha Cha
  • Table Ronde
  • Naweli Boboto
  • Merengue Scoubidou

Indépendance Cha Cha became one of the most recognised musical expressions of African independence. Its influence extended well beyond the Congo.

1961: Rebuilding and New Sessions

After major personnel changes, Grand Kallé rebuilt the orchestra. New sessions brought together established musicians and younger performers, including Tabu Ley and Manu Dibango during this wider period of collaboration.

  • Jamais Kolonga
  • Africa Bola Ngombi

1962–1963: The Surboum African Jazz Period

Through the Surboum African Jazz label and publishing venture, Kabasele and Roger Izeidi helped create an important platform for Congolese recording artists. The catalogue included recordings by different musical formations.

  • Matanga Ya Mobido
  • Kamulanga
  • Bana Na Nwa
  • Nabongisa Kala
  • Tika Ndeko Na Yo Te
  • Mama Ngai Habanera
  • African Jazz Mokili Mobimba
  • Miwela-Miwela
  • Bolingo Suka Te

The Surboum catalogue is particularly significant because it documented a wider recording community, not only one fixed African Jazz lineup.

Research note: Planet Ilunga reports at least 212 songs associated with African Jazz formations in the label's 1960–1963 catalogue. This is a recording catalogue figure, not a count of albums or proof that every recording was released exclusively under the African Jazz name.

1963–1966: A New African Jazz Formation

Following the 1963 split, Grand Kallé continued with a reconstituted African Jazz. The orchestra's later recordings reflected a changing membership and musical direction.

  • Jolie Nana
  • Nzambe E Mungu
  • Makwela Ya Bana Mboka

These recordings belong to the later African Jazz period. They should be distinguished from the separate African Fiesta orchestras formed by musicians who left in 1963.

1966–1969: Later Recordings

African Jazz continued to record as its membership evolved. In 1966, Kabasele also worked with musicians in Paris on the temporary African Team de Paris project.

  • Butsana Mama
  • Carrefour Addis Ababa – sometimes associated with the title Indépendance Cha Cha II.
  • Lipopo Ya Banganga
  • Kinshasa Makambo – a title associated with the later catalogue.
  • Moselebende De Bolingo
  • Mbombo Ya Tshimbalanga
  • BB 69 – associated with the orchestra's late period.

Note: Some histories place the end of African Jazz earlier, while other accounts document later recordings and activity. The late 1960s are therefore presented here as a period of transition rather than a universally agreed final date.

1970–1971: The African Team Project

African Team was a later project involving Grand Kallé and musicians including Manu Dibango. It should not be confused with the original African Jazz orchestra or the temporary African Team de Paris sessions of 1966.

  • Ko Ko Ko... Qui Est Là?
  • Suzi Na Edo
  • Africa Boogaloo
  • Safari Muzuri
  • Nzela Mosika
  • Merengue Fontaine
  • Biwela Biwela

The project explored a blend of Congolese rumba and Afro-Cuban dance influences, including charanga, mambo, pachanga, merengue and boogaloo.

Important Compilation: Kallé Chauffez Bruxelles!

Kallé Chauffez Bruxelles! is a modern anthology that brings together selected recordings from the Brussels and wider African Jazz story.

It is useful for discovering important recordings, but it should not be treated as the complete original discography. The anthology also includes material connected with Manu Dibango's musical formations.

Understanding the Recording Credits

  • Some early recordings involved studio musicians whose identities were not consistently documented.
  • Personnel changed frequently, so a musician's appearance on a recording does not always prove permanent membership.
  • Song titles may have different spellings across labels, reissues and later catalogues.
  • Composer credits on modern compilations may differ from original label information.
  • Recordings released under the Surboum label should not automatically be attributed to one fixed African Jazz lineup.
