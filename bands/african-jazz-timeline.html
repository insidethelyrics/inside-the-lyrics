African Jazz: Historical Timeline

From the early 1950s to the African Team recordings of 1970–71

Important events, musical developments, recordings and changes in the history of Grand Kallé's orchestra.

African Jazz did not remain the same orchestra throughout its history. Its membership, recording arrangements and musical direction changed considerably over time.

This timeline follows the major stages of its development, including the early Opika recordings, the Esengo period, the Brussels journey, the establishment of Surboum African Jazz, the 1963 breakaway and the later African Team recordings.

Some dates differ between historical accounts. Where the evidence is uncertain, the timeline identifies the difference rather than presenting one version as definitive.

1950–1954

Early musical development

  • 1950: Joseph Kabasele begins recording with musicians including Georges Doula.
  • Early recordings include Valerine Regina.
  • Around 1951: African Jazz begins to take shape in Léopoldville.
  • 1952: Musicians including Tino Baroza, Albert Taumani and other instrumentalists become associated with Kabasele's developing musical circle.
  • 1953: The orchestra is generally recognised as having been officially established.
  • 1954: Isaac Musekiwa is among the musicians associated with the orchestra's recording history.

This period established the foundations of the orchestra's sound and Grand Kallé's position as a leading musical organiser.

1955–1956

Growing musical presence

  • African Jazz continues developing its repertoire and recording activity.
  • The orchestra expands its presence beyond Léopoldville.
  • By 1956, African Jazz is performing in parts of the Belgian and French Congo.
  • The orchestra is associated with performances at Faignond in Brazzaville.

African Jazz was becoming part of a wider urban music scene, with audiences extending beyond its home city.

1957

The Opika closure and Esengo transition

  • Opika closes after its owners return to Greece.
  • African Jazz becomes associated with Esengo through Henri Bowane.
  • Musicians from other musical formations contribute to the changing recording environment.
  • Philippe Lando (Rossignol), Jean-Serge Essous, Jean Malapet, Roitelet, Saturnin Pandi and Roger Izeidi are among the names connected with this era.
  • Baila, composed by Jean-Serge Essous, becomes an important recording associated with the period.

1958

Changes in musical identity and personnel

  • The relationship between African Jazz and Rock-a-Mambo remains part of the period's complicated recording history.
  • By the middle of the year, Rock-a-Mambo is operating as a separate musical group.
  • Kabasele re-establishes the African Jazz identity.
  • Changes in personnel include the departure of Tino Baroza and a greater reliance on Déchaud and Dr Nico on guitars.

The orchestra continues developing its sound while musicians move between different recording and performance groups.

1959

New vocal talent

  • 6 June 1959: Tabu Ley is presented at a Vis-à-vis concert.
  • Tabu Ley begins his association with the musical environment surrounding African Jazz.
  • The orchestra continues building its reputation in the Congolese music scene.

Tabu Ley would later become one of the most important figures to emerge from African Jazz's history.

1960

The Brussels journey and Congolese independence

  • 20 January: The Brussels Round Table discussions begin.
  • 28 January: African Jazz performs a farewell concert at Lisala Bar in Léopoldville.
  • 29 January: The orchestra departs for Europe, travelling through Paris.
  • 30 January: The group arrives in Brussels.
  • 1 February: African Jazz gives its first official Brussels performance at Hotel Plaza.
  • The orchestra performs more than 60 concerts across Belgium, France and the Netherlands, according to historical accounts.
  • Indépendance Cha Cha becomes an internationally recognised independence anthem.
  • Kabasele and Roger Izeidi negotiate with Fonior and establish the Surboum African Jazz recording and publishing operation.
  • September: Major personnel changes affect the orchestra. Vicky Longomba and André Menga become associated with Negro Succès, while Brazzos returns to OK Jazz.
  • 30 June: The Congo becomes independent.

The year 1960 remains one of the most significant chapters in the history of African Jazz.

1961

Reorganisation and new recordings

  • Kabasele rebuilds the orchestra following the previous year's departures.
  • June: African Jazz returns to Brussels for another recording period.
  • Musicians associated with this formation include Edo Lutula, Joseph Mwena, Roger Izeidi, Tabu Ley, Tino Baroza and Dicky Baroza.
  • Manu Dibango becomes involved with African Jazz following his connection with Kabasele in Brussels.
  • Recordings include Jamais Kolonga, Africa Bola Ngombi, Matanga Ya Mobido and Kamulanga.

The Brussels sessions helped maintain the orchestra's recording activity despite continuing changes in its membership.

1962

West African tour and continued recording

  • African Jazz continues producing recordings for the Surboum catalogue.
  • November: The orchestra begins a West African tour lasting approximately ten weeks.
  • The itinerary includes Bamako, Ouagadougou, Dakar, Monrovia, Abidjan, Lagos and Port Harcourt.
  • Information 3171 is among the recordings associated with the orchestra's 1962 activity.

The tour strengthened African Jazz's presence in the wider African music circuit.

1963

The African Fiesta breakaway

  • May: A major split takes place within African Jazz.
  • Roger Izeidi, Dr Nico and Tabu Ley are among the musicians who depart.
  • The departing musicians establish African Fiesta.
  • Kabasele rebuilds African Jazz with new personnel.
  • Recordings associated with the reorganised orchestra include Jolie Nana, Nzambe E Mungu and Makwela ya Bana Mboka.

The split marked the beginning of a new chapter in Congolese music. African Fiesta would later produce further important musical formations.

1964–1965

The post-split African Jazz

  • African Jazz continues operating under Grand Kallé's leadership.
  • The orchestra works with a reconstituted group of singers and instrumentalists.
  • Papa Noël is associated with this post-split period before his departure.
  • Jeannot Bombenga later leaves and becomes associated with Vox Africa.

These years demonstrate the orchestra's effort to maintain its musical identity after losing several established performers.

1966

African Team de Paris

  • Kabasele reconnects in Paris with Manu Dibango and Jean-Serge Essous.
  • A temporary African Team de Paris session project takes shape.
  • Recordings include Merengue Fontaine and Biwela Biwela.
  • African Jazz also continues its activity in Kinshasa.

The Paris project is an important collaboration, but it should not be confused with the later African Team ensemble of 1970–1971.

1967–1968

Later musical activity

  • 1967: Kabasele records music associated with the Organisation of African Unity conference.
  • One account describes the production of 38 versions of a commemorative song, although the details of the recordings require further verification.
  • 1968: African Jazz records Moselebende de Bolingo.
  • Mbombo ya Tshimbalanga is also associated with the later recording period.

1969

BB 69 and the end of the orchestra's active period

  • BB 69, associated with a reworking of Mathieu Kouka's song Bébé, becomes a notable late success.
  • Mathieu Kouka leaves following disagreements over payment.
  • Further musician departures weaken the orchestra.
  • Ken Braun's account places the end of African Jazz's active operations towards the end of the year.

Dating note: Some histories give an earlier ending date for African Jazz. This timeline follows the detailed account documenting later recordings, including the 1968 material and BB 69.

1970–1971

The later African Team recordings

  • Grand Kallé works with a larger African Team formation.
  • The ensemble includes Manu Dibango, Edouard Lutula, Joseph Mulamba (Mujos), Jean Munsi (Kwamy) and Don Gonzalo Fernandez.
  • Its music combines Congolese rumba with charanga, mambo, pachanga, merengue and boogaloo.
  • Recordings include Ko Ko Ko... Qui Est Là?, Suzi na Edo, Africa Boogaloo and Safari Muzuri.
  • Planned album releases do not materialise as originally intended. Recordings appear in fragmented single releases.

These recordings represent a later chapter in Grand Kallé's career and the continuing international reach of Congolese popular music.

Understanding the Dates

This timeline distinguishes the early development of African Jazz from its official establishment, the 1960 personnel departures from the 1963 split, and the 1966 Paris sessions from the later African Team recordings.

The dates represent the best-supported chronology in the research currently assembled. Some personnel periods, song credits and final activity dates remain subject to further discographical verification.
