African Jazz

The Orchestra That Helped Shape Modern Congolese Rumba

Founded under the leadership of Joseph Kabasele, popularly known as Grand Kallé.

In the musical history of the Congo, few orchestras occupy a position as important as African Jazz. Under the leadership of Grand Kallé, the orchestra became one of the pioneering forces in the development and popularisation of modern Congolese dance music.

Its story stretches from the early 1950s through the years of political independence, international performances, record-label changes, musician departures and the emergence of new musical formations.

African Jazz was more than a musical group. It was part of a changing cultural landscape in which Congolese musicians developed a distinctive urban sound by combining local musical traditions with Cuban and other international influences.

African Jazz at a Glance

Leader and founder Joseph Athanase Tshamala Kabasele (Grand Kallé)
Location Léopoldville, Belgian Congo (present-day Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo)
Early beginnings Around 1951
Official establishment 1953
Musical identity Congolese rumba and modern African dance music
Historic recording Indépendance Cha Cha (1960)
Major offshoot African Fiesta (1963)
Later project African Team (1966 and 1970–71 formations)

1. The Beginnings: 1951–1953

African Jazz emerged in Léopoldville during a period of considerable change in urban Congolese music. Joseph Kabasele was among the musicians seeking to establish a modern orchestra capable of bringing together local musical expression and contemporary dance arrangements.

The orchestra's early development is associated with approximately 1951, while 1953 is generally given as the year of its official establishment.

Before becoming a widely recognised orchestra leader, Kabasele had worked as a typist. His interest in music brought him into contact with the Opika recording studio, where he developed his recording career.

His early recording activity included work with musicians such as Georges Doula. These early studio collaborations belong to the background of his musical development, but they should not automatically be treated as formal African Jazz membership.

Historical distinction: Some accounts place the orchestra's beginnings in 1951, while others identify 1953 as its official establishment. Both dates are retained here because they describe different stages of its development.

2. The Opika Years

Opika played an important role in the early recording history of African Jazz. It provided a recording environment in which Kabasele and other musicians developed their sound and built an audience.

The orchestra's early musical identity grew from a combination of Congolese rhythms, Lingala singing, guitar-based arrangements and influences from Cuban and Latin American music.

Among the early musicians associated with this period were Marcelin Laboga, Charles Mwamba (Déchaud), Georges Doula, Albert Yamba-Yamba and other instrumentalists and vocalists.

Nicolas Kasanda, widely known as Dr Nico, became one of the important guitarists associated with the orchestra's development.

Early recordings and titles associated with this broader period include Valerine Regina, Kale Kato and Parafifi.

3. The Esengo Years: 1957–1959

In 1957, the closure of Opika brought a major change to the Congolese recording environment. African Jazz subsequently became associated with Esengo, a label connected to Henri Bowane.

This period also brought musicians from different musical backgrounds into contact with African Jazz. Among the important names associated with the recordings were Philippe Lando, known as Rossignol, Jean-Serge Essous, Jean Malapet, Augustin Moniania (Roitelet), Saturnin Pandi and Roger Izeidi.

The importance of Baila

Baila, composed by Jean-Serge Essous, became one of the notable recordings associated with this period.

Its personnel included Grand Kallé and Rossignol on vocals, Tino Baroza and Dr Nico on guitars, Essous on clarinet, Nino Malapet on saxophone, Roitelet on bass, Pandi on congas and Izeidi on maracas.

Other titles associated with the Esengo period include Tujala Tshibemba, Mokonzi Ya Mboka and Tembe Nye.

Changes in the orchestra

The late 1950s were not a period of fixed membership. Musicians moved between recording groups and orchestras, and the boundaries between different musical formations were sometimes fluid.

The relationship between African Jazz and Rock-a-Mambo during this era is an example of why individual recording credits and group identities need to be examined carefully.

4. The Brussels Journey: 1960

One of the defining moments in African Jazz history came in 1960, when the orchestra travelled to Belgium during the period of the Congo's political transition to independence.

Congolese organisers Thomas and Philippe Kanza arranged for African Jazz to provide a musical presence during the Brussels Round Table discussions.

The Brussels delegation

The special touring formation included:

  • Grand Kallé — vocals and leadership
  • Vicky Longomba — vocals
  • Dr Nico — solo guitar
  • Déchaud — rhythm guitar
  • Brazzos — bass
  • Roger Izeidi — maracas
  • Petit Pierre — percussion

The group held a farewell performance at Lisala Bar in Léopoldville on 28 January 1960. It departed for Europe the following day, travelling through Paris before arriving in Brussels on 30 January.

Its first official Brussels performance took place at the Hotel Plaza on 1 February 1960.

Indépendance Cha Cha

The orchestra's Brussels period became closely associated with Indépendance Cha Cha, a recording that grew into one of the most recognisable musical expressions of African independence.

The song's popularity extended beyond the Congo. It became associated with the wider independence movement across the African continent.

Other recordings connected with this period include Table Ronde, Naweli Boboto, Mawonso Pamba and Merengue Scoubidou.

The Congo became independent on 30 June 1960. African Jazz's music had become part of the soundtrack of this historic period.

5. Surboum African Jazz and the Recording Business

The Brussels journey also opened an important business chapter for Grand Kallé and Roger Izeidi. Their negotiations with Fonior led to the creation of the Surboum African Jazz label and publishing operation.

Surboum became an important platform for Congolese music. It was not limited to African Jazz recordings: other performers and orchestras, including Franco's OK Jazz, also recorded music associated with the label.

This distinction matters when examining its catalogue. A recording released through Surboum should not automatically be described as an African Jazz performance.

Research by Planet Ilunga identifies at least 212 African Jazz recordings across different formations for the label between 1960 and 1963. This is a catalogue figure, not a count of albums.

The label's history therefore belongs to the wider story of Congolese recording and music entrepreneurship, as well as to the story of African Jazz itself.

6. Departures, Reorganisation and New Tours: 1960–1962

The orchestra experienced major personnel changes after the Brussels journey. In September 1960, disagreements and departures reshaped its membership.

Vicky Longomba and André Menga became associated with the formation of Negro Succès. Brazzos returned to OK Jazz, while Dr Nico and Déchaud also departed from the principal Kabasele-led formation.

Kabasele continued rebuilding African Jazz. During a return journey to Brussels in 1961, the orchestra recorded extensively with musicians including Edo Lutula, Joseph Mwena, Roger Izeidi, Tabu Ley, Tino Baroza and Dicky Baroza.

Manu Dibango also became connected with African Jazz through Kabasele's Brussels contacts. His involvement with the Congolese music scene included touring and recording activity during the early 1960s.

Recordings of the period

Important titles associated with the 1961–1962 period include:

  • Jamais Kolonga
  • Africa Bola Ngombi
  • Matanga Ya Mobido
  • Kamulanga
  • Bana Na Nwa
  • Nabongisa Kala
  • Tika Ndeko Na Yo Te
  • Mama Ngai Habanera
  • African Jazz Mokili Mobimba
  • Miwela-Miwela
  • Bolingo Suka Te

The West African tour

In November 1962, African Jazz undertook a major West African tour lasting approximately ten weeks. The itinerary included Bamako, Ouagadougou, Dakar, Monrovia, Abidjan, Lagos and Port Harcourt.

The tour demonstrated the orchestra's reach beyond Central Africa and its place within a growing network of African popular music.

7. The Great Split: 1963

May 1963 marked one of the most consequential turning points in African Jazz history. A major breakaway occurred following disagreements involving management, finances and the musicians' working arrangements.

Roger Izeidi, Dr Nico and Tabu Ley were among the musicians who left the Kabasele-led orchestra. The departing musicians went on to establish African Fiesta.

The separation also involved a dispute over the African Jazz name. Kabasele maintained his claim to the orchestra's identity, while the departing musicians adopted a new name.

African Fiesta would later experience its own divisions, leading to the emergence of African Fiesta National under Tabu Ley and African Fiesta Sukisa under Dr Nico.

Important distinction: The 1960 personnel departures and the major 1963 breakaway were separate events. They should not be treated as one continuous split.

8. Rebuilding African Jazz: 1963–1966

Following the 1963 breakaway, Grand Kallé reconstituted African Jazz with a new generation of musicians.

The reorganised orchestra included singers Jeannot Bombenga, Mathieu Kouka and Jean Nsita, alongside guitarists such as André Kambite (Damoiseau) and Casimir Mutshipule (Casino). Papa Noël was also associated with this period.

The new formation continued recording and performing despite the loss of several prominent musicians.

Titles associated with this period include Jolie Nana, Nzambe E Mungu and Makwela ya Bana Mboka.

Personnel continued to change. Papa Noël departed within a relatively short period, and Jeannot Bombenga later left to rebuild Vox Africa with Papa Noël.

9. The Final Years: 1966–1969

During the second half of the 1960s, African Jazz continued to record under changing circumstances. Grand Kallé also worked with musicians outside the regular Kinshasa orchestra.

African Team de Paris: 1966

In 1966, Kabasele reconnected in Paris with Manu Dibango and Jean-Serge Essous. Their collaboration produced recordings associated with a temporary project known as African Team de Paris.

Among the recordings associated with this session project were Merengue Fontaine and Biwela Biwela.

This 1966 project should not be confused with the later, larger African Team formation active around 1970–1971.

Later recordings

African Jazz continued producing music in Kinshasa. Recordings associated with the later period include:

  • Butsana Mama
  • Carrefour Addis Ababa
  • Lipopo ya Banganga
  • Kinshasa Makambo
  • Moselebende de Bolingo
  • Mbombo ya Tshimbalanga
  • BB 69

The recording BB 69, associated with a reworking of Mathieu Kouka's song Bébé, became an important late success.

Disagreements and further musician departures weakened the orchestra. Ken Braun's historical account describes African Jazz as ceasing operations towards the end of 1969.

10. African Team: 1970–1971

The African Team recordings of 1970–1971 represent a later chapter in Grand Kallé's musical career.

This larger formation brought together Kabasele, Manu Dibango, Edouard Lutula, Joseph Mulamba (Mujos), Jean Munsi (Kwamy), Cuban flautist Don Gonzalo Fernandez and additional instrumentalists.

Its sound drew on a variety of dance styles, including charanga, mambo, pachanga, merengue, boogaloo and Congolese rumba.

Recordings

Titles associated with the African Team recordings include:

  • Ko Ko Ko... Qui Est Là?
  • Suzi na Edo
  • Africa Boogaloo
  • Safari Muzuri
  • Nzela Mosika
  • Merengue Fontaine
  • Biwela Biwela

The intended album did not appear in the form originally planned. Recordings were instead released in fragmented formats, including 45 rpm singles through different labels and publishing arrangements.

Research note: Some discographical accounts place the beginning of African Team in 1969, while other sources focus on its 1970–1971 recording activity. The 1966 Paris sessions and the later ensemble must remain distinct in this archive.

11. The Legacy of African Jazz

African Jazz occupies a central place in the history of modern Congolese popular music. Under Grand Kallé, it helped develop a sound that travelled beyond the Congo and became part of a wider African musical identity.

Its musicians, recordings and internal transformations also shaped the histories of other important orchestras. The departure of musicians did not end their influence; many went on to lead or join new musical formations.

The orchestra's story is therefore also a story of musical exchange, entrepreneurship, artistic disagreement and the movement of musicians across the African popular music scene.

From the early Opika recordings to the Brussels performances and the later African Team sessions, African Jazz left a body of work that continues to deserve careful documentation and listening.
