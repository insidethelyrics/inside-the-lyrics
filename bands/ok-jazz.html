🎸 OK Jazz

OK Jazz became one of the defining musical institutions of Congolese popular music. Its story is closely connected with guitarist, singer and bandleader Franco Luambo and with the generation of musicians who helped shape the sound of modern Congolese rumba.

📌 OK Jazz at a Glance

Band OK Jazz
Formation 6 June 1956
Central Figure Franco Luambo
Early Recording Connection Loningisa
Musical Tradition Congolese Rumba and African Popular Music

🕰️ Formation — 6 June 1956

OK Jazz emerged on 6 June 1956 around Franco Luambo and a group of musicians associated with the recording scene of the time.

The early group brought together guitarists, vocalists, percussionists and wind instrumentalists. Some equipment was rented, while some percussion equipment was made by the musicians themselves.

🎵 The Birth of the Name "OK Jazz"

One of the earliest references to the name OK Jazz on record appeared in the title of Franco's composition "La Rumba OK."

The band's name was given a fuller introduction through "On Entre OK, On Sort KO", composed by Franco. The phrase can be understood as: "You enter OK, you leave knocked out."

The recording featured:

  • Vocals: Franco, Vicky Longomba and Rossignol
  • Guitars: Franco and De La Lune
  • Congas: Bosuma Dessoin

The first record identified in our archive as carrying the band's name on its label was "Etali Ye C'is", backed by "Collette", credited to Franco and Essous accompanied by OK Jazz.

📀 1957 — The First Major Recording Period

In 1957, OK Jazz released more than a dozen records for Loningisa.

One of the notable early recordings was "Pasi Ya Boloko" ("Suffering in Prison"), associated with Pandy, a drummer from Brazzaville.

According to the historical material in our archive, the song drew from Pandy's personal experience after he was imprisoned by Belgian authorities over unpaid tax.

The musical environment surrounding OK Jazz was diverse. Merengue, bolero, cha-cha-cha, charanga, pachanga, tango and beguine were among the styles popular during the era.

🔄 Changes in the Band — 1957

1957 brought several important personnel changes.

Rossignol Leaves

Lead vocalist Laudot Philippe "Rossignol", also known as "The Nightingale", left the group. Edo Nganga subsequently joined as a vocalist.

Historical accounts in our source material connect Rossignol's departure with competition between him and Vicky Longomba over the lead-vocal position.

Other Changes

  • Willy Mbemba — Trumpet
  • Nino Malapet — temporarily filled the trumpet role
  • Isaac Musekiwa — later joined on saxophone
  • Jean Serge Essous — later departed OK Jazz

🌊 The Brazzaville Connection

OK Jazz maintained strong musical links with Brazzaville. In 1957, Vicky Longomba took the band across the Malebo Pool to Brazzaville, where the group stayed at Macedo Bar for several months.

During this period, additional musicians joined the expanding ensemble.

  • Kouka Célestine — Vocals
  • Edo Lutula — Clarinet
  • Armando Antoine "Brazzos" — Rhythm Guitar

⛓️ Franco's Imprisonment

Franco was arrested and imprisoned at Ndolo Prison after violating traffic regulations. He was later transferred to Luzumu Prison outside the city.

During his imprisonment, Franco composed "Mukoko."

The song was connected to Willy Mbemba, the OK Jazz trumpeter who was also imprisoned. Franco's song reflected the circumstances surrounding Mbemba's imprisonment.

After Franco's release, OK Jazz had travelled to Mbandaka in the Equatorial region. Franco followed the group by river steamer.

This journey inspired another version, "Mukoko W' Masumbuku," dedicated to the captain of the steamer on which Franco travelled.

🎷 1958 — Expanding Musical Influences

In 1958, OK Jazz recorded "Ah Bolingo Pasi" ("Oh, Pain of Love").

Senegalese musicians associated with M'Bengue et Son Cha Cha Cha Boys de Dakar also visited Congo during this period.

Franco's interest in jazz and other international musical influences formed part of the wider musical development surrounding OK Jazz.

🇨🇩 Music During the Independence Era

As political tensions increased in the Congo, popular music increasingly reflected the country's changing social and political environment.

In January 1959, disturbances broke out in Leopoldville following the intervention of colonial authorities at a political meeting involving Joseph Kasavubu and Patrice Lumumba.

Franco responded to the political atmosphere with "Kodi Yaya," described in our source material as a political song associated with the ABAKO movement.

🌍 1960 — Europe and the Independence Conference

In January 1960, Grand Kallé and African Jazz travelled to Europe with delegates attending the Round Table Conference concerning Congolese independence.

Several musicians connected to OK Jazz were part of this historic journey, including:

  • Nico Kasanda
  • Edo Lutula
  • Armando "Brazzos"
  • Vicky Longomba

🎤 OK Jazz Without Vicky Longomba

In 1960, Vicky Longomba was still performing with Grand Kallé. Franco consequently took much of the lead vocal responsibility within OK Jazz.

Franco later recruited Mulamba Joseph "Mujos", a singer whose alto voice helped maintain a vocal character similar to Vicky's.

🎸 Simaro Lutumba Joins OK Jazz

In 1960, Simaro Lutumba joined OK Jazz from Orchestra Micra for recording sessions in Brussels, playing rhythm guitar.

After the recording trip, Simaro temporarily left the group but later returned and became an important figure in the band's history.

Leon Bolhen, a lead guitarist, later joined after Brazzos had departed to continue working with Grand Kallé.

🇨🇬 1960 — Congo-Brazzaville Independence

The independence of Congo-Brazzaville in 1960 had a major effect on OK Jazz.

Several musicians from Brazzaville returned home, including:

  • Daniel Loubelo "De La Lune"
  • Edo Nganga
  • Célestine Kouka

They subsequently became associated with Les Bantous de la Capitale, alongside other musicians including Jean Serge Essous.

The movement of musicians between the two Congos became an important feature of Central African musical history.

🎼 1961 — A New Generation

In 1961, Piccolo Ishiamala replaced De La Lune on bass guitar.

Another important arrival was Verckys Kiamuangana Mateta, who joined OK Jazz during this period.

More from 1961 will be added as the OK Jazz archive continues.

🎺 1962 – The Orchestra Expands

In 1962, OK Jazz undertook its first foreign tour, accompanying delegates to a political convention in Nigeria. The tour coincided with the inauguration of a new airline service between Léopoldville and Lagos.

Dele Pedro joined OK Jazz as a tenor saxophone player. He settled with the band, married Cheri Zozo and became a long-serving member. He composed Tu Bois Beaucoup (You Drink a Lot).

The band also composed two songs:

  • Amida Asukisi Malato – Amida Dresses Best.
  • Cherie Zozo – composed in honour of a great fan of the band.

De La Lune returned to OK Jazz in 1962. Vicky Longomba also returned after his period with Grand Kalle. These developments helped create one of the strongest line-ups the orchestra had yet assembled.

OK Jazz was later registered as a business with the Chamber of Commerce.

🎼 1963 – The TP OK Jazz Identity

In 1963, Edo Nganga and Simaro reappeared with the band. Lola Checain joined as a vocalist, while Michael Boyibanda also joined around this period.

By this time, OK Jazz had acquired the prefix TP, meaning Tout Puissant. According to the source notes, the title was taken from TP Engelbert and intended to eclipse the rank of Seigneur, the mock-religious title associated with Rochereau.

🎸 The Formation of African Fiesta

In 1963, Dr. Nico and his brother Dechaud broke away from Kabasele to form African Fiesta with Rochereau, described in the source notes as Kalle's singing protégé.

Disagreements between the two vocalists and the guitarist eventually led to the formation of two separate groups:

  • African Fiesta National – led by Rochereau.
  • African Fiesta Sukisa – led by Dr. Nico.

🎙️ 1964–1965 – Financial Struggles and Political Change

1964: The Financial Crisis

In 1964, OK Jazz was crippled by debt. Franco reportedly had to hide the band's instruments from bailiffs.

That same year, OK Jazz recorded Marie Ngai Marie Nzoto Ebebe, described in the source notes as one of the orchestra's most successful songs. It was a satirical song about modern morality.

1965: A Changing Political Environment

In 1965, Colonel Mobutu took control of the nation in a coup.

The source notes state that musicians were obliged to pay homage to the new president. Franco recorded Au Commandement (To Authority).

Leading musical groups began receiving state sponsorship. Musicians from different regions were also brought together for presidential functions.

Groups mentioned in connection with this period include:

  • Vox Africa
  • African Jazz
  • African Fiesta

Vox Africa, founded in 1963, later gave rise to Festival des Maquisards and Grand Maquisards. The group also had a major hit in 1965 called Jeanine, sung by Sam Mangwana.

In 1965, Youlou Mabiala and Celi Bitchoumanou, known as Bitchou, joined OK Jazz.

Around this period, Luvumbu Ndoki was recorded and banned, according to the source notes.

🌍 1966 – The World Festival of Black Arts

In 1966, OK Jazz was invited to the World Festival of Black Arts held in Dakar, Senegal.

On his return journey from Senegal, Franco travelled through Brussels to purchase equipment. He left Verckys Kiamungana in charge of the Kinshasa group.

During this period, Franco also made recordings at Studio Boulogne in Paris with a group of students, including Flavien Kasavubu, son of the first president. The source notes say that Flavien's contribution remained anonymous. The recordings were released under the name Orchestra Franco.

⚡ The Orchestra Revolution Breakaway

On returning to Kinshasa, Franco discovered that more than nine OK Jazz musicians had defected during his absence.

Kwamy Munsi, who had left the band earlier, had encouraged musicians to join the aptly named Orchestra Revolution.

The musicians specifically mentioned in the source notes were:

  • Piccolo – bass player
  • Brazzos – rhythm guitarist
  • Mujos – vocalist
  • Djeskin – vocalist
  • Jojo – vocalist

Mujos had reportedly returned to OK Jazz a few months earlier and contributed to the 1965 hit Dix Makuta.

The departure led to a musical exchange between Franco and Kwamy:

  • Chicotte (The Whip) – Franco's attack on Kwamy.
  • Faux Millionaire (False Millionaire) – Kwamy's response, satirising the habits of the nouveau riche and their financial troubles.
  • Cource au Pouvoir (Race for Power) – Franco's concluding response.

🎸 1967–1970 – New Recruits and Orchestra Vévé

1967: The Orchestra Faces More Changes

In 1967, while OK Jazz was returning from Brazzaville, port officials inspected the band's equipment after the musicians disembarked from a ferry. They reportedly recovered an amplifier that had been reported stolen.

Franco intervened on behalf of the band. The musicians were briefly held while the matter was resolved.

During another trip to Europe, Verckys, who had joined OK Jazz in 1962, began forming his own orchestra.

In late 1967, while Franco was again in Europe buying equipment, Verckys invited several OK Jazz musicians to record a series of songs for release under his own name on the Ecodis label.

Those mentioned in the source notes include:

  • Simaro
  • Bitchou
  • Checain
  • Youlou
  • Moce Fan Fan of Orchestra Revolution

After the recordings, Verckys travelled to Europe to have the discs pressed.

Franco later returned to Kinshasa and encouraged musicians who had left OK Jazz to return.

Other important developments in 1967 included:

  • Moce Fan Fan joined OK Jazz.
  • Verckys left OK Jazz to form Orchestra Vévé.
  • Randot Kasongo wa Kasongo joined from Negro Succes.

The song Marcelina is also associated with this period in the source notes.

1969–1970: Further Musical Developments

In 1969, James Brown visited Kinshasa.

The songs Minoko (The Mouth) and Edo Aboyi Ngai (Edo Rejects Me) were recorded around this period.

In 1970, Kwamy Munsi made a comeback to OK Jazz.

Vicky Longomba left OK Jazz in 1970 and joined Conga Succes before later moving to Orchestra Lovy.

Kwamy Munsi died in 1974.

🎤 1971 – The Francolettes and New Trumpeters

In 1971, after days of mourning following the death of Baron Marie Marie, Franco returned to the orchestra.

He recruited the Rocherettes and renamed them the Francolettes.

Four new trumpeters were recruited:

  • Barami Miranda
  • Adamo Seye
  • Kapitena Kasongo
  • Vieux Kalloux

The recruitment made it possible for Franco to revisit songs that Simaro, Bitchou and Fan Fan had recorded with Verckys. According to the source notes, Franco retrieved the recordings and had them reworked.

  • Infedelite Mado – Bitchou
  • Ma Hell (My Helen) – Simaro

Ma Hell presented a difficult challenge to Franco.

🎶 1972–1973 – Sam Mangwana, Josky and Wuta Mayi

1972: Changes in Membership

In 1972, Bitchou was dismissed. The source notes describe him as a difficult and argumentative character.

Youlou Mabiala also left OK Jazz in 1972.

Moce Fan Fan left OK Jazz and joined Vicky's Orchestra Lovy. He began regrouping with Youlou, Bitchou and Kwamy in SOMO SOMO.

Other arrivals and changes included:

  • Armando Brazzos rejoined OK Jazz after three years with Orchestra Revolution.
  • Mpudi Decca joined from Orchestra Vedette.
  • Sam Mangwana joined from African Fiesta via Maquisards.

Mangwana's first songs with OK Jazz included:

  • Mabeli (The Bark)
  • Où est le sérieux (Where Is the Seriousness?)
  • Luka Mobali Moko (Look for One Man) – his debut composition with the band.

1973: New Voices and Guitarists

In 1973, Josky Kiambukuta joined OK Jazz from Orchestra Continental. The song AZDA was released that year.

Mayaula Mayoni joined as a songwriter and rhythm guitarist after ending his football career with Vita Club.

Blaise Mayanda, known as Wuta Mayi, joined as a vocalist.

Other musicians recruited in 1973 included:

  • Thierry Mantuika – guitarist from Thu.
  • Gege Mangaya – from Zaina.

The source notes record two football matches between Vita Club and Ashanti Kotoko:

  • 25 November 1973 – Ashanti Kotoko 4–2 Vita Club.
  • 16 December 1973 – Vita Club 0–3 Ashanti Kotoko.

🎼 1974–1976 – SOMO SOMO, New Arrivals and Honours

1974: SOMO SOMO

In 1974, the Rumble in the Jungle boxing match between Muhammad Ali and George Foreman took place.

That year, Moce Fan Fan formed Orchestra SOMO SOMO with Youlou Mabiala, Celi Bitchou and Kwamy.

Financial difficulties led to the group's break-up:

  • Youlou returned to OK Jazz.
  • Celi joined Orchestra Vévé.
  • Kwamy joined Tabu Ley.
  • Fan Fan travelled through Zambia, Tanzania and Kenya before going to England.

The source notes place Ndombe Opetum's arrival from Tabu Ley's band in 1974, replacing Sam Mangwana.

Other musicians who joined in 1974 included:

  • Mavituki "Visi" Michelino – solo guitarist from Africa International.
  • Empompo "Deyesce" Loway – saxophonist.

1975: Sam Mangwana's International Career

In 1975, Sam Mangwana left OK Jazz to pursue an international career. After travelling through West Africa and Côte d'Ivoire, he joined musicians who had left Afrisa International to form African All Stars.

The musicians named in the source notes include:

  • Dizzy Mandjeku
  • Lokassa ya Mbongo
  • Bopol Mansiamina
  • Pablo Lubadika
  • Syran Mbenza

Henri Bowane became the manager of Sam Mangwana's African All Stars.

1976: National Recognition

In 1976, Franco was honoured with the National Order of the Leopard. Each member of the band also received a medal for contributions to the arts of Zaire.

In September 1976, Ntesa Dalienst and Jerry Dialunguna joined from Les Grands Maquisards. Lokombe Ntal and Dizzy Mandjeku also joined during 1976.

Ntesa initially left after a short stay of approximately two months. In November 1976, Franco pleaded with him to return, and he rejoined the orchestra.

His first songs with the band are listed as:

  1. Tala ye Na Mico – Don't Mind Him.
  2. Zalina Mopaya – Zaina the Stranger.
  3. Lisslo ya Adamo wa Nzambe – A Dialogue Between Adam and God.

🌍 1977 – OK Jazz at FESTAC in Lagos

In 1977, OK Jazz was selected to represent its country at FESTAC in Lagos, Nigeria.

The source notes state that Zaire's four days in the main event were divided between OK Jazz and Afrisa.

Rochereau introduced some members of Zaïko Langa Langa, while OK Jazz was accompanied by Ray Lema. The orchestra also introduced M'Pongo Love, a 21-year-old female singer who had originally been coached by saxophonist Empompo Loway.

Between 1977 and 1978, two vocalists joined OK Jazz:

  • Dialu Lukoki, known as Dialtho.
  • Djo Mpoyi Kaninda.

In 1978, Papa Noël Nedule also joined OK Jazz.

🎼 Other Historical Notes

The source notes identify Bavon Marie Marie as an original member of Orchestra Jamel Jazz, founded in 1967 by saxophonist Empompo Loway, known as Deyesce.

The notes also describe Franco's relationship with Annie Mbule, whom he reportedly met during a tour in Bangui, Central African Republic. Annie was a dancer and later became his wife and the mother of several of his children.

The source separately mentions Annie Marie Paulina, who parted ways with Franco and later became a businesswoman.

In October 1971, President Mobutu introduced a cultural revolution. The country's name changed from Congo-Kinshasa to Zaire, and the river and currency were also renamed.

The slogan recorded in the notes was: One Country, One River, One Currency.

A major musical festival was held at a stadium to launch the new national anthem, La Zairoise. The source notes attribute its composition to Lutumba (not Simaro), Joseph Luvum.

⚖️ 1978 – “Jacky”, “Helena” and the Prison Episode

In 1978, Franco composed two songs, Jacky and Helena. The songs were considered pornographic and led to the arrest of Franco and several OK Jazz musicians.

Musicians Taken to Makala Prison

  2. Mackos
  3. Thierry
  4. Flavien
  5. Jerry
  6. Gege
  7. Papa Noël
  8. Kapitena
  9. Musekiwa
  10. Checain

A few days later, Franco's presence reportedly proved disruptive, and he was transferred to Luzumu Prison in Bas-Zaire.

After 22 days, the musicians were released following a presidential pardon. Franco remained detained for another 11 days after being taken to hospital with a fever.

Ntesa Dalienst avoided imprisonment. He acknowledged participating in the singing but argued that he had not uttered the obscene words.

When challenged, he reportedly answered that Franco had already been making music in that manner since 1956, before Ntesa had joined the band.

The source notes also connect Attention na Sida to this episode. The song reused guitar chords from Jacky and Helena, turning the musical material from a morally controversial song into one with a morally upright message.

🧵 “Tonga Abotoli Tonga Naye” – The Tailleurs Story

After his release, Franco returned to the studio and composed Tonga Abotoli Tonga Naye, also known as Tailleurs.

The title is translated in the source notes as “The Owner of the Needle Has Taken Back His Needle”. The song was interpreted as a satirical commentary on the appointment and subsequent demotion of Kengo wa Dondo as Prime Minister. The notes say Kengo had pushed for Franco's imprisonment.

🕯️ 12 October 1989 – The Death of Franco

Franco died on 12 October 1989 at Mont Yvoir, Belgium, at approximately 11:00 a.m. He had travelled to Belgium for medical treatment.

According to the source notes, the people who accompanied him on his instructions were:

  1. Makoso
  2. Mpudi Decca
  3. Randot

A former Vita Club and Congo national-team player named Kibonge, who lived in Brussels, informed Makoso to meet the Congolese ambassador, identified in the notes as Kimbulu.

Makoso was then informed of Franco's death and passed the news to Madilu and the other musicians.

Those named in the source notes as being at the hospital included:

  • Annie Mbuli – Franco's wife.
  • General Matondo.

When President Mobutu received the news, he ordered Franco's body to be flown back to Kinshasa immediately.

Most OK Jazz musicians were reportedly not in Brussels or Kinshasa at the time. Many were in the Netherlands working on their own projects.

The arrangements for the return journey were difficult because of the urgency. The source notes mention that air tickets had been paid for and that one ticket was to be issued as a passenger boarded the plane.

When Franco's health deteriorated, Simaro bought an air ticket to travel from Kinshasa to Belgium. Franco died on the same day Simaro travelled, meaning Simaro could not attend Franco's burial.

🥁 Bakuba Mayopi Orchestra

Bakuba Mayopi Orchestra was formed by Seskain Molenga.

Madilu joined the orchestra in 1971. In 1972, the group broke up.

Following the break-up:

  • Pepe Kalle joined Bella Bella.
  • Papy Tex joined Bella Mambo.

Tosta Taluki later called Madilu back after a disagreement with Molenga. Bakuba Mayopi was revived with Pires and Jose Dilu.

Later, Dilu joined Kalle and Papy Tex in creating Empire Bakuba.

In 1974, Madilu briefly joined Lipua Lipua before returning to Mayopi. The band eventually collapsed.
