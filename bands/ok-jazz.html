🎸 OK Jazz

OK Jazz became one of the defining musical institutions of Congolese popular music. Its story is closely connected with guitarist, singer and bandleader Franco Luambo and with the generation of musicians who helped shape the sound of modern Congolese rumba.

📌 OK Jazz at a Glance

Band OK Jazz
Formation 6 June 1956
Central Figure Franco Luambo
Early Recording Connection Loningisa
Musical Tradition Congolese Rumba and African Popular Music

🕰️ Formation — 6 June 1956

OK Jazz emerged on 6 June 1956 around Franco Luambo and a group of musicians associated with the recording scene of the time.

The early group brought together guitarists, vocalists, percussionists and wind instrumentalists. Some equipment was rented, while some percussion equipment was made by the musicians themselves.

🎵 The Birth of the Name "OK Jazz"

One of the earliest references to the name OK Jazz on record appeared in the title of Franco's composition "La Rumba OK."

The band's name was given a fuller introduction through "On Entre OK, On Sort KO", composed by Franco. The phrase can be understood as: "You enter OK, you leave knocked out."

The recording featured:

  • Vocals: Franco, Vicky Longomba and Rossignol
  • Guitars: Franco and De La Lune
  • Congas: Bosuma Dessoin

The first record identified in our archive as carrying the band's name on its label was "Etali Ye C'is", backed by "Collette", credited to Franco and Essous accompanied by OK Jazz.

📀 1957 — The First Major Recording Period

In 1957, OK Jazz released more than a dozen records for Loningisa.

One of the notable early recordings was "Pasi Ya Boloko" ("Suffering in Prison"), associated with Pandy, a drummer from Brazzaville.

According to the historical material in our archive, the song drew from Pandy's personal experience after he was imprisoned by Belgian authorities over unpaid tax.

The musical environment surrounding OK Jazz was diverse. Merengue, bolero, cha-cha-cha, charanga, pachanga, tango and beguine were among the styles popular during the era.

🔄 Changes in the Band — 1957

1957 brought several important personnel changes.

Rossignol Leaves

Lead vocalist Laudot Philippe "Rossignol", also known as "The Nightingale", left the group. Edo Nganga subsequently joined as a vocalist.

Historical accounts in our source material connect Rossignol's departure with competition between him and Vicky Longomba over the lead-vocal position.

Other Changes

  • Willy Mbemba — Trumpet
  • Nino Malapet — temporarily filled the trumpet role
  • Isaac Musekiwa — later joined on saxophone
  • Jean Serge Essous — later departed OK Jazz

🌊 The Brazzaville Connection

OK Jazz maintained strong musical links with Brazzaville. In 1957, Vicky Longomba took the band across the Malebo Pool to Brazzaville, where the group stayed at Macedo Bar for several months.

During this period, additional musicians joined the expanding ensemble.

  • Kouka Célestine — Vocals
  • Edo Lutula — Clarinet
  • Armando Antoine "Brazzos" — Rhythm Guitar

⛓️ Franco's Imprisonment

Franco was arrested and imprisoned at Ndolo Prison after violating traffic regulations. He was later transferred to Luzumu Prison outside the city.

During his imprisonment, Franco composed "Mukoko."

The song was connected to Willy Mbemba, the OK Jazz trumpeter who was also imprisoned. Franco's song reflected the circumstances surrounding Mbemba's imprisonment.

After Franco's release, OK Jazz had travelled to Mbandaka in the Equatorial region. Franco followed the group by river steamer.

This journey inspired another version, "Mukoko W' Masumbuku," dedicated to the captain of the steamer on which Franco travelled.

🎷 1958 — Expanding Musical Influences

In 1958, OK Jazz recorded "Ah Bolingo Pasi" ("Oh, Pain of Love").

Senegalese musicians associated with M'Bengue et Son Cha Cha Cha Boys de Dakar also visited Congo during this period.

Franco's interest in jazz and other international musical influences formed part of the wider musical development surrounding OK Jazz.

🇨🇩 Music During the Independence Era

As political tensions increased in the Congo, popular music increasingly reflected the country's changing social and political environment.

In January 1959, disturbances broke out in Leopoldville following the intervention of colonial authorities at a political meeting involving Joseph Kasavubu and Patrice Lumumba.

Franco responded to the political atmosphere with "Kodi Yaya," described in our source material as a political song associated with the ABAKO movement.

🌍 1960 — Europe and the Independence Conference

In January 1960, Grand Kallé and African Jazz travelled to Europe with delegates attending the Round Table Conference concerning Congolese independence.

Several musicians connected to OK Jazz were part of this historic journey, including:

  • Nico Kasanda
  • Edo Lutula
  • Armando "Brazzos"
  • Vicky Longomba

🎤 OK Jazz Without Vicky Longomba

In 1960, Vicky Longomba was still performing with Grand Kallé. Franco consequently took much of the lead vocal responsibility within OK Jazz.

Franco later recruited Mulamba Joseph "Mujos", a singer whose alto voice helped maintain a vocal character similar to Vicky's.

🎸 Simaro Lutumba Joins OK Jazz

In 1960, Simaro Lutumba joined OK Jazz from Orchestra Micra for recording sessions in Brussels, playing rhythm guitar.

After the recording trip, Simaro temporarily left the group but later returned and became an important figure in the band's history.

Leon Bolhen, a lead guitarist, later joined after Brazzos had departed to continue working with Grand Kallé.

🇨🇬 1960 — Congo-Brazzaville Independence

The independence of Congo-Brazzaville in 1960 had a major effect on OK Jazz.

Several musicians from Brazzaville returned home, including:

  • Daniel Loubelo "De La Lune"
  • Edo Nganga
  • Célestine Kouka

They subsequently became associated with Les Bantous de la Capitale, alongside other musicians including Jean Serge Essous.

The movement of musicians between the two Congos became an important feature of Central African musical history.

🎼 1961 — A New Generation

In 1961, Piccolo Ishiamala replaced De La Lune on bass guitar.

Another important arrival was Verckys Kiamuangana Mateta, who joined OK Jazz during this period.

More from 1961 will be added as the OK Jazz archive continues.

🌍 1962 — The First Foreign Tour and a Stronger Line-up

In 1962, OK Jazz undertook what the notebook describes as its first foreign tour. The orchestra accompanied delegates travelling to a political convention in Nigeria. The journey also coincided with the inauguration of the new airline service between Leopoldville and Lagos.

The same year brought important changes to the band's personnel. Dele Pedro joined as a tenor saxophonist. He settled into the orchestra, married Cheri Zozo and became one of its long-serving members. The notebook attributes the composition "Tu Bois Beaucoup" ("You Drink a Lot") to him.

The archive also records that OK Jazz was registered as a business with the Chamber of Commerce. Two songs associated with this period are "Amida Asukisi Malato" ("Amida Dresses Best") and "Cherie Zozo", composed in honour of a great fan of the band.

Daniel Loubelo, known as De La Lune, returned to OK Jazz in 1962. Vicky Longomba also returned after completing his period with Grand Kalle. The notebook describes the resulting group as one of the strongest line-ups the orchestra had assembled.

🎤 1963 — The TP OK Jazz Identity

In 1963, Edo Nganga and Simaro Lutumba reappeared in the OK Jazz story. Lola Checain joined as a vocalist, and Michael Boyibanda is recorded in the notebook as joining during 1963, although the accompanying line-up labels his arrival as 1964. That date difference is retained rather than silently resolved.

By this period the orchestra was using the prefix TP, short for Tout Puissant. The notebook explains that the title was adopted in reference to TP Engelbert and was intended to project a status above the title "Seigneur" associated with Rochereau.

Outside OK Jazz, 1963 also saw Dr. Nico and his brother Dechaud break away from Kalle's musical circle and establish African Fiesta with Rochereau. The notebook records that disagreements among the musicians later led to two separate groups: African Fiesta National, associated with Rochereau, and African Fiesta Sukisa, associated with Dr. Nico.

💿 1964 — Financial Pressure and a Major Recording

The notebook describes 1964 as a difficult financial period for OK Jazz. The orchestra was reportedly crippled by debt, and Franco had to hide musical instruments from bailiffs.

Despite those difficulties, the band recorded "Marie Ngai Marie Nzoto Ebebe", described in the notes as one of its most successful songs. The title is glossed in the notebook as a reference to "Marie, whose body is crumbling"; the song is characterised there as satire on modern morality.

🏛️ 1965 — Political Change, State Functions and New Recruits

In 1965, Colonel Mobutu took power in a coup. The notebook records that musicians were expected to pay homage to the new president. Franco's song "Au Commandement" ("To Authority") is placed in this political context.

The notes state that leading musical groups received state sponsorship and that musicians from different regions were brought together for presidential functions. Groups named in this context include Vox Africa, African Jazz and African Fiesta.

Vox Africa, founded in 1963 according to the notebook, had a major 1965 hit, "Jeanine", sung by Sam Mangwana. The notes also connect Vox Africa's later musical family with Festival des Maquisards and Grand Maquisards.

Two important recruits joined OK Jazz in 1965: Youlou Mabiala and Celi Bitchoumanou ("Bitchou"). The notebook places the recording of "Luvumbu Ndoki" in this period and says that it was banned.

Another song associated with this period is "Dix Makuta", to which the notes later connect the returning vocalist Mujos.

🌍 1966 — Dakar's World Festival of Black Arts

In 1966, OK Jazz was invited to the World Festival of Black Arts in Dakar, Senegal. After the festival, Franco travelled back through Brussels to purchase equipment and left Verckys Kiamuangana in charge of the Kinshasa group.

During this period Franco also made recordings at Studio Boulogne in Paris with a group of students. The notebook names Flavien Kasavubu, son of the first president, among those involved, while noting that the student's composition and contribution remained anonymous. These recordings were released under the name Orchestra Franco.

The Orchestra Revolution Split

When Franco returned to Kinshasa, he discovered that more than nine OK Jazz musicians had defected during his absence. The notebook identifies Kwamy Munsi as a central figure in drawing musicians into the breakaway Orchestra Revolution.

The musicians specifically named in the notes include:

  • Piccolo — bassist
  • Armando Brazzos — rhythm guitarist
  • Mujos — vocalist, who had returned to the band and contributed to "Dix Makuta"
  • Djeskin — vocalist
  • Jojo — vocalist

The split produced a musical exchange through songs. Franco composed "Chicotte" ("The Whip"), directed at Kwamy. Kwamy answered with "Faux Millionaire" ("False Millionaire"), satirising the habits and debts of the nouveau riche. Franco then responded with "Course au Pouvoir" ("Race for Power").

🎷 1967 — Verckys, Orchestra Vévé and the Return of Musicians

In 1967, OK Jazz encountered a problem during a journey from Brazzaville. Port officials inspecting the band's equipment after the ferry crossing reportedly found an amplifier that had been reported stolen. Franco stepped forward on behalf of the orchestra. The group was briefly held while the matter was resolved.

Verckys, who had joined OK Jazz in 1962, began establishing his own orchestra. In late 1967, while Franco was again in Europe purchasing equipment, Verckys invited several musicians connected to OK Jazz to record songs under his own name for the Ecodis label.

The notebook names the following participants:

  • Simaro Lutumba
  • Celi Bitchoumanou
  • Lola Checain
  • Youlou Mabiala
  • Mose Fan Fan, then associated with Orchestra Revolution

After the recordings, Verckys travelled to Europe to have the discs pressed. Franco later returned to Kinshasa and encouraged musicians who had left to come back.

Mose Fan Fan joined OK Jazz in 1967. That same year, Verckys left to establish Orchestra Vévé.

Also in 1967, Randot Kasongo wa Kasongo joined OK Jazz from Negro Succes. The notebook also mentions the song "Marcelina" in this period, describing its lyrics as using striking, magical imagery.

🎶 1968–1969 — Recording Activity and a Visit by James Brown

The notebook does not provide a separate detailed annual chronology for 1968. For 1969, it records that James Brown visited Kinshasa.

Songs placed around this period in the archive include "Minoko" ("The Mouth") and "Edo Aboyi Ngai" ("Edo Rejects Me").

The notes also say that in 1969 Fan Fan recorded with Simaro, Bitchou, Youlou and Empompo Loway. In 1970, this group scored a song titled "Mele", named after Franco's personal assistant Jean-Jean, with Orchestra Mi.

🎙️ 1970 — Kwamy's Return and Vicky's Departure

In 1970, Kwamy Munsi made a comeback to the OK Jazz line-up.

The same year, Vicky Longomba left OK Jazz. The notebook says that he joined Conga Succes and later Orchestra Lovy.

The archive's line-up notes for the early 1970s include Franco, Vicky, Roitelet, Liberlin, La Monta, Bosuma Dessoin, Lièvre, Isaac Musekiwa, Edo Lutula, Verckys, Dele Pedro, Edo Nganga, Simaro, Lola Checain, Michael Boyibanda, Youlou Mabiala, Celi Bitchou, Randot Kasongo wa Kasongo, Mose Fan Fan and Kwamy. The list is a period snapshot and should not be read as proof that every musician was present at precisely the same time.

🥁 1971 — The Francoelettes and a New Brass Section

The notebook records that after days of mourning following the death of Baron Marie Marie, Franco returned to musical activity. He recruited the Rocherettes and renamed them the Francoelettes.

Four trumpeters were recruited from Rochereau's musical circle: Barami Miranda, Adamo Seye, Kapitena Kasongo and Vieux Kalloux.

Their recruitment enabled Franco to revisit recordings that Simaro, Bitchou and Fan Fan had made with Verckys. According to the notes, Franco retrieved and reworked the material. Two songs specifically named are "Infedelite Mado", associated with Bitchou, and "Ma Hell" ("My Helen"), associated with Simaro.

The notes also describe an early-1970s period when Franco was absent and Mose Fan Fan took charge of the band. Fan Fan's compositions "Djemalasi" and "Mongando" are identified as major hits.

🔄 1972 — Departures, Returns and New Arrivals

In 1972, Celi Bitchoumanou was dismissed. The notebook describes him as difficult and argumentative. Youlou Mabiala also left OK Jazz that year.

Mose Fan Fan left and joined Vicky's Orchestra Lovy. The notes connect Fan Fan, Youlou, Bitchou and Kwamy with the regrouping that became SOMO SOMO.

Several musicians joined or rejoined OK Jazz in 1972:

  • Armando "Brazzos" rejoined after three years with Orchestra Revolution.
  • Mpudi Decca joined from Orchestra Vedette.
  • Sam Mangwana joined from African Fiesta via the Maquisards.

The notebook identifies "Mabeli" ("The Bark") and "Où est le sérieux" ("Where Is the Seriousness?") as among Mangwana's first songs to sing with OK Jazz. His debut composition for the orchestra is given as "Luka Mobali Moko" ("Look for One Man").

🎼 1973 — Josky Kiambukuta, Mayaula Mayoni and a Growing Orchestra

In 1973, Josky Kiambukuta joined OK Jazz from Orchestra Continental. The notebook also records the release of "AZDA".

Mayaula Mayoni, formerly a footballer with Vita Club, joined as a songwriter and rhythm guitarist. The notes connect his move into music with Vita Club's 3–0 home defeat by Ghana's Ashanti Kotoko. The notebook includes the match dates and scores: Ashanti Kotoko beat Vita Club 4–2 on 25 November 1973, and Vita Club lost 0–3 at home on 16 December 1973.

Other recruits listed for 1973 are Blaise Mayanda ("Wuta Mayi") as a vocalist, Thierry Mantuika, described as a guitarist from Thu, and Gege Mangaya, whose source note reads "Zaina".

🌐 1974–1975 — SOMO SOMO, Returning Musicians and International Careers

In 1974, the Rumble in the Jungle boxing match between Muhammad Ali and George Foreman took place in Zaire. The notebook places the formation of SOMO SOMO in this period under guitarist Mose Fan Fan, with Youlou Mabiala, Celi Bitchou and Kwamy among its members.

Financial problems contributed to SOMO SOMO's break-up. The notebook records the subsequent movements as follows:

  • Youlou Mabiala returned to OK Jazz.
  • Celi Bitchou joined Orchestra Vévé.
  • Kwamy joined Tabu Ley's group.
  • Mose Fan Fan travelled through Zambia, Tanzania and Kenya before going to England.

The notes also record several important additions to OK Jazz in 1974:

  • Ndombe Opetum joined from Tabu Ley's band, replacing Sam Mangwana.
  • Mavituki "Visi" Michelino joined from Africa International and is described as a master of solo guitar.
  • Empompo "Deyesce" Loway joined as a saxophonist.

In 1975, Sam Mangwana left OK Jazz to pursue an international career. The notebook says he travelled to West Africa and Côte d'Ivoire and joined musicians who had left Afrisa International to form All African Stars.

The musicians named in that group are:

  • Dizzy Mandjeku
  • Lokassa ya Mbongo
  • Bopol Mansiamina
  • Pablo Lubadika
  • Syran Mbenza

Henri Bowane is identified in the notes as manager of Sam Mangwana's African All Stars.

🏅 1976 — National Recognition and New Members

In 1976, Franco was honoured with the National Order of the Leopard. The notebook says that members of the band also received medals in recognition of their contribution to the arts of Zaire.

In September 1976, Ntesa Dalienst and Jerry Dialunguna joined OK Jazz after the collapse of Les Grands Maquisards.

The notebook associates Ntesa with three early songs: "Tala Ye Na Mico" ("Don't Mind Him"), "Zalina Mopaya" ("Zaina the Stranger") and "Lisolo ya Adamo wa Nzambe", described as a dialogue between Adam and God.

Other musicians listed as joining in 1976 include Lokombe Ntal and Dizzy Mandjeku. Youlou Mabiala is recorded as having rejoined in 1974.

🌍 1977 — OK Jazz at FESTAC in Lagos

In 1977, OK Jazz was selected to represent Zaire at FESTAC, the Second World Black and African Festival of Arts and Culture, held in Lagos, Nigeria. The notebook says Zaire's four days of main event performances were shared between OK Jazz and Afrisa.

Rochereau is described as introducing members of Zaiko Langa Langa, while OK Jazz appeared with Ray Lema and introduced the singer M'pongo Love. The notes describe her as a young, physically disabled singer who had originally been coached by saxophonist Empompo Loway.

The archive records two vocalists joining around 1977–1978: Dialu Lukoki ("Dialtho") and Djo Mpoyi Kaninda. Djo Mpoyi Kaninda is specifically recorded as a vocalist.

⚖️ 1978 — Papa Noël Joins and the "Jacky" Case

In 1978, guitarist Papa Noël Nedule joined OK Jazz.

The notebook records that Franco composed "Jacky" and "Helena" during this period. The songs led to his arrest because authorities considered their lyrics obscene. Several OK Jazz musicians were also taken to Makala Prison.

The musicians named in the notebook are:

  1. Simaro
  2. Mackos
  3. Thierry
  4. Flavien
  5. Jerry
  6. Gege
  7. Papa Noël
  8. Kapitena
  9. Musekiwa
  10. Checain
  11. Franco

The notes say that Franco's presence at Makala became disruptive and he was transferred to Luzumu Prison in Bas-Zaire. The other musicians were released after approximately 22 days following a presidential pardon. Franco remained detained for a further 11 days after being taken to hospital with a fever.

Ntesa Dalienst is recorded as having avoided imprisonment by arguing to the prosecutor that, although he had taken part in the singing, he had not uttered the obscene words.

The notebook connects "Attention na Sida" with the musical chords of "Jacky" and "Helena", describing it as a transformation from a morally controversial song into a morally upright one.

"Tailleurs" and the Aftermath

After his release, Franco returned to the studio and composed "Tonga Abotoli Tonga Naye", also known as "Tailleurs". The notebook interprets it as a satirical commentary connected with Kengo wa Dondo's appointment and subsequent demotion as prime minister, and with the political pressure that had preceded Franco's imprisonment.

📚 1979–1988 — The Continuing OK Jazz Story

The surviving notebook transcription available for this restoration does not provide a detailed year-by-year account for 1979 through 1988. Rather than invent tours, recordings, personnel changes or events, this section marks the gap honestly.

The wider OK Jazz story continued during these years. Additional details can be incorporated from further handwritten notes, original record labels, contemporary publications and other historical sources.

🕯️ 1989 — The Death of Franco Luambo

The notebook records that Franco Luambo died on 12 October 1989 at Mont Yvoir in Belgium, at approximately 11:00 a.m. It says he had travelled to Belgium for medical treatment.

According to the notes, the people who followed Franco to Europe at his instruction included Makoso, Mpudi Decca and Randot. A former Vita Club and Congo national-team player known as Kibonge, who was living in Brussels, informed Makoso to meet the Congolese ambassador, Kimbulu. Makoso was then informed of Franco's death and passed the news to Madilu and other members of the musical circle.

The notebook identifies Annie Mbuli, described there as Franco's wife, and General Matondo among those present at the hospital. It also records that President Mobutu ordered Franco's body to be flown back to Kinshasa immediately.

Many OK Jazz musicians were not in Brussels at the time. The notes say that several members were in the Netherlands pursuing their own projects, while others were not in Kinshasa. This created difficulties in arranging travel in time for the return and funeral.

Simaro Lutumba, who had remained in Kinshasa, bought an air ticket to travel to Belgium when Franco's condition deteriorated. The notebook says that Franco died on the same day Simaro travelled, meaning Simaro could not attend Franco's burial.

Franco's death marked the end of the era led by the musician who had been the central figure of OK Jazz since its formation in 1956. The orchestra's story, however, did not end with this chapter.

📝 Editorial Notes on the Restored Archive

This expanded chronology follows the surviving Inside the Lyrics notebook transcription. A few inconsistencies remain visible in the source:

  • Michael Boyibanda is described as joining in 1963 in one passage, while the 1963 line-up labels him 1964.
  • Roitelet / Roitalet is said to have left in 1960, but he appears in several later line-up lists.
  • Ntesa Dalienst's 1976 departure and return are described in more than one way.
  • Names such as Liberlin / Libertin, Lola Checain / Chacain, and Dialu Lukoki / Dialtho vary in the notes.
  • Some line-up lists do not specify instruments or exact membership dates.

These variations have not been silently reconciled. They should be checked against original record labels, contemporary publications and other independent sources before being treated as settled facts.
